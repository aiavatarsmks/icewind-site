How much does a website cost in the UK?

Typical 2026 prices for small-business sites, bespoke builds, e-commerce and web apps, what moves a quote up or down, and how to budget for what comes after launch.

By Maksim Ilin, AI Consultant at ICE WIND · 15+ years in software engineering · Updated 13 October 2026

Short answer: it depends on what the site has to do. What you pay is set by the type of site, how many pages and features it needs, who writes the content, what it has to connect to, and how much support you want after launch. As a guide, most UK businesses pay between £1,500 and £8,000 for a professionally built website in 2026. E-commerce stores run £4,500 to £15,000, and custom web products with logins, dashboards and payments start around £8,000 and climb past £25,000. A studio that gives you a firm number before asking what the site has to do is guessing.

This guide breaks those ranges down: what affects the cost, what each type of project includes, the running costs that arrive after launch, and how to plan a budget that holds up once real quotes come in.

What affects website cost in the UK?

Six things decide most of a quote. The first four are about the build itself, the last two about what surrounds it.

The biggest is the type of site. A one-page site, a ten-page business site and a shop with a checkout are different jobs, not bigger and smaller versions of the same one.

Next comes scope. Ten pages need ten designs, ten rounds of copy and ten passes of testing. Page count is the easiest lever to pull when a budget is tight.

Then the design approach. A template skin on WordPress or Shopify costs a fraction of a design built around your customers. Bespoke web design pays off when you need to stand out or turn cold traffic into enquiries. A template works fine for a site people only visit after they have already met you.

Content is the one people forget: somebody has to write the pages. Professional copywriting adds £500 to £2,000 on most projects, and late content is the most common reason a launch slips.

Then features and integrations: booking, payments, a member area, a CRM connection. Hooking up a booking or accounting tool through an off-the-shelf plugin usually costs little, while custom API work adds £500 to £3,000 or more per integration. Unplanned integrations cause more mid-project price surprises than anything else, so name them early.

Finally, support after launch. Hosting, updates, small changes and security are running costs rather than part of the build. They are covered further down.

Design and development are separate bills

“Website design prices” and “web development prices” get used as if they meant the same purchase. Design is deciding how the site is structured, what each page has to do and how it looks. Development is turning that into something that works, loads fast and keeps working. On most projects development is the larger bill, and the gap widens as the site gets more technical. That is why “just make it look like this site” quotes come in low and then climb: the look was never the expensive part. Our web development page sets out what the build side covers.

Did AI make websites cheaper?

Partly. AI tooling cut the hours behind repetitive work such as boilerplate code, first-draft layouts and test coverage, and at ICE WIND it is a real part of why our landing page price starts at £1,500 rather than £3,000. It did not make cheaper the parts that decide whether a site works: understanding your customers, structuring the offer, writing pages that sell. Fully AI-generated sites exist, cost almost nothing, and read like it.

One-page / brochure / small-business website

At the bottom of the range, £1,500 to £2,500 buys one well-built page: your copy and photos, a layout designed for a single action (call, book or send the form), fast and correct on a phone. You do not get a content system you can edit yourself or anyone rewriting your text. For a trade business that wins work by phone, one strong page often does more than ten pages nobody reads.

A brochure site of five to ten pages, £3,500 to £8,000, is where most UK small businesses land, and it is the honest answer to the question of what website design costs a small business. For that you should get a design made for you rather than adapted from a template, service pages that stand a chance of ranking, and someone thinking about how a stranger gets from your homepage to an enquiry. Copy help is usually included at this level, or at least editing. Our small business web design page shows what that involves.

If you only want one number, it is about £5,000: roughly the middle of what a UK small business pays for a site that is expected to bring in enquiries. Use it as a sanity check rather than a budget. A one-page site for a plumber at that price is overpriced; a booking platform at that price is a warning.

Website with bespoke design, CMS and integrations

The price climbs when your team needs to edit the site, when it holds a lot of content, or when it has to talk to other systems. A content-managed site runs £5,000 to £12,000. The money goes on modelling your content (services, case studies, team, locations) so it can be edited without breaking the layout, on moving old content across, and on each integration: CRM, booking, newsletter, accounting.

This is also where bespoke design earns its cost. A business that sells on trust, or competes for the same search terms as twenty others, usually gets more from a design built around its customers than from a popular theme with the colours changed.

E-commerce, web app or custom platform

Past £8,000 you are rarely buying a website any more. You are buying software with pages attached. E-commerce stores sit at £4,500 to £15,000 because product pages, basket, checkout, payments, shipping rules and VAT handling are each real build work. Web products with logins, dashboards and payments start around £8,000 and climb past £25,000. At this level the first thing to agree is a scoped first version with milestones, not a single number for “the platform”.

Ongoing costs: hosting, maintenance, content, SEO, integrations

A website costs money after launch, and a build quote rarely shows it. Plan for these from day one:

  • Domain: £10 to £20 a year.
  • Hosting: from a few pounds a month for a static site to £50 or more for a busy store.
  • Maintenance and small changes: £30 to £300 a month on a typical care plan, covering updates, backups, security fixes and the odd text change.
  • Content: new pages, case studies and seasonal updates, whether you write them or pay for them.
  • SEO: a site does not rank because it launched. If search is meant to bring in customers, ongoing SEO work is a separate budget line.
  • Integrations and licences: booking tools, CRM seats, premium plugins, fonts and email tools. Monthly fees add up quietly.

Across UK guides, £500 to £2,000 a year is a realistic running total for a small business site before any SEO or content work. That is roughly £40 to £170 a month, and most of it is maintenance rather than hosting. Static sites sit at the bottom of that range, which is one reason we default to them for projects that do not need a heavy CMS.

When a lower upfront price becomes more expensive later

Under £1,000 usually buys a template with your logo dropped in, built quickly by someone who may not be around in six months. That is fine as a placeholder while you test an idea. When the site has to earn its keep, the saving tends to come back as a bill in one of four ways:

  • The rebuild: a site that cannot be extended gets replaced, and the second build costs more than doing it properly once, because it also has to carry the old site’s URLs and rankings across. Our website redesign page explains what that involves.
  • Lost enquiries: a slow page, or a form nobody can find on a phone, costs more each month than the saving.
  • Security clean-ups: sites parked on a cheap plan with nobody looking after them are the ones that get hacked, and clearing that up costs more than a year of care would have.
  • Lock-in: some cheap builds keep the domain, the hosting account or the design files in the supplier’s name. Check who owns what before you pay.

DIY builders sit in the same trade-off. Wix or Squarespace costs about £9 to £30 a month on an annual plan and suits a side project. Our comparison of Wix or Squarespace against a custom website covers the point where the builder stops being the cheap option.

How to plan a realistic website budget

Start from what the site has to do rather than from a page count. A site that must bring in enquiries from search needs a different budget from one people visit after a referral.

  1. Write down the one job the site must do, and how you will know it is working.
  2. List the pages and features you need now, and separately the ones that can wait for a second phase.
  3. Decide who writes the content. If it is you, set yourself a deadline before the build starts.
  4. Add a year of running costs to the build price, plus 20% VAT if the supplier is VAT-registered and you cannot reclaim it.
  5. Keep some budget back for changes you will only spot once you see the site working.

Freelancer, agency or studio?

A freelancer typically charges £1,200 to £2,000 for a standard four to five page site in the UK. Good freelancers are excellent value; the risks are availability and breadth, because one person rarely covers design, build, SEO and support equally well. An agency or studio costs more because you get a process and more than one specialist. UK and US senior developer time bills at roughly $120 to $200 an hour according to OneLittleWeb’s 2026 pricing study. Whoever you hire, check the company is real: registration at Companies House, insurance, and an ICO registration if they handle personal data. We publish ours on our trust page.

Does a London studio cost more?

A little, and less than you might expect. Most studios, ours included, quote a client in Cardiff the same as one in Shoreditch, because it is the same work. Location only adds cost when you want people in a room for workshops or a launch.

How an accurate quote is put together

Vague briefs get padded quotes. A studio prices risk, so the less it knows, the more margin it adds. This is how we turn a brief into a fixed price, and roughly what you should expect from any careful supplier.

It starts with discovery: a short conversation about the goal, who the site is for, the pages and features, and what already exists. If you have a current site we look at it first, and sometimes the right answer is a smaller job than you expected.

Then scope: a written list of pages, features and integrations, who supplies the content, and what is explicitly out of scope. This is the document the price is fixed against.

Then milestones: design, build, content, testing and launch, each with a date and a review point. At ICE WIND we fix the cost once the scope is agreed and split payment 50% at the start and 50% before delivery.

That is why an accurate number depends on the task rather than on a price list. To get one, send a short brief through start a project: your goal, a couple of reference sites, the pages or features you need, your deadline and your budget band.

FAQ

Common questions about website costs

How much does a website cost in the UK?
It depends on the type of site and its scope. As a guide for 2026, a single landing page from a studio runs £1,500 to £3,500, a five to ten page business website £3,500 to £8,000, an e-commerce store £4,500 to £15,000, and a custom web product from around £8,000 upwards. A fixed price comes once the scope is agreed.
What is included in website design pricing?
Usually the structure of the site, page designs for desktop and mobile, and one or two rounds of revisions. Ask whether copywriting, imagery, the build itself, content entry and launch are included, because a quote labelled website design sometimes covers only the design files.
How much does web development cost in the UK?
Development is usually the larger part of the bill. For a brochure site it is included in the ranges above; custom features cost more, and a custom API integration typically adds £500 to £3,000 or more. Web products with logins, dashboards and payments start around £8,000 and can pass £25,000.
What ongoing website costs should I budget for?
Domain, hosting, maintenance, licences and any paid tools the site connects to. Budget £500 to £2,000 a year for a small business site, more if you add SEO or regular content. Ask for the running costs in writing before you sign the build contract.
Is a cheap website always cheaper in the long term?
No. A site under £1,000 can be the right placeholder, but if it cannot be extended, loads slowly or is left unmaintained, the rebuild, the lost enquiries or the security clean-up usually cost more than building it properly once.
How can I get an accurate quote?
Write a one-page brief: the goal, a couple of reference sites, the pages and features you need, your deadline and your budget band. A studio can then agree a written scope with you and give a fixed price instead of a range.
What is the average cost of website design for a small business in the UK?
About £5,000 for a small business site that has to bring in enquiries. It is a midpoint rather than a target: a single landing page costs a third of that, and anything with logins or payments costs several times more.
How long does a website take to build?
A landing page takes one to two weeks. A 5–10 page business site takes three to five weeks. E-commerce and web apps run four to twelve weeks depending on integrations. Content is the usual bottleneck, so start writing early.
Does a website cost more in London?
Barely, and less than it used to. Remote work flattened the difference, and most studios quote the same wherever the client is. Location only adds cost when you want people in a room for workshops or a launch.
Is VAT included in UK website quotes?
Usually not. UK agencies quote ex-VAT, so add 20% if the supplier is VAT-registered and you cannot reclaim it. Ask directly, as this turns a £5,000 quote into £6,000.
Do I pay extra for a mobile version?
No. Any site built since roughly 2015 is one site that adapts to the screen, and the mobile layout is part of the quote. If a quote lists a mobile version as an extra, that is a warning about who wrote it.
